The five vowels

Vōcālēs

Similar mouth shape, different duration. English keywords are approximations, not perfect matches.

The "no glide" rule. English o in "note" secretly ends in a w; English ey in "they" drifts toward ee. Latin ō and ē don't drift. Lock the mouth in place and hold it steady.

Diphthongs

Diphthongī

Two vowels, one syllable, one continuous glide. All diphthongs count as long.

ae eye — as in "aisle" caelum · puella → puellae · maestus
oe oy — as in "boy" poena · proelium · foedus
au ow — as in "how" audit · nauta · aurum
eu eh + oo, run together — not "yoo" heu · Eurōpa · seu
ei ay — as in "eight" deinde · hei
ui oo-ee, said fast huic · cui · huius
Where the two systems split hardest. Ecclesiastical (Church) Latin flattens both ae and oe into a plain "ay." Classical keeps them as true diphthongs — so caelum rhymes with "eye," not with "chay."

When ae & oe aren't diphthongs

Diaeresis

Sometimes each vowel keeps its own syllable. The macron usually gives it away.

āēr AH-ehr — two syllables not the "eye" sound
poēta po-EH-ta — three syllables o and ē belong to separate syllables

That final -a pair is the one that bites people constantly in LLPSI. Train your ear on it early and a lot of case confusion sorts itself out.

The short i argument

Disputātiō

You'll find contradicting answers on whether the "ih" of pit exists in Latin. Here's the state of play.

Why the mainstream view says yes

Allen's Vox Latina — the standard reference for classical pronunciation — holds that short i differed from long ī in quality, not just length: laxer and more open. He recommends the pit vowel as the practical approximation for English speakers.

The strongest evidence is what happened next. In the Romance languages, short i didn't merge with ī — it merged with long ē:

  • fidem → Italian fede
  • vīta → Italian vita
  • Same with u: bucca → bocca, but lūna → luna

Roman spelling also wobbled — optimus/optumus, maximus/maxumus — which only makes sense if the vowel sat somewhere murky in the middle.

Where the other answer comes from

Two places, mostly. Ecclesiastical pronunciation is Italianate, and Italian has no lax i at all — so in that system short i really is just a clipped [i]. And some classical teachers prefer a tidy "five vowels, length only" model because it's easier to teach and stops English speakers from mangling everything else.

Sardinian gets cited too: it merged Latin's vowels by length alone. It's a real data point, though most scholars read it as a later local development rather than evidence about Cicero's mouth.

Practical takeaway. Use the pit vowel — it's solid, defensible ground. Just don't lean so hard it sounds like an impression. Length is the load-bearing feature; quality is the small stakes.
